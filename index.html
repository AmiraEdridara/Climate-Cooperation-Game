The Climate Cooperation Game

Watch the world's 12 biggest emitters decide whether to cooperate or defect — on a real world map, against 33 years of real data.

Climate change is the textbook example of a collective action problem. Everyone benefits if emissions fall, but each country individually benefits more if they keep emitting while others cut.

This site walks through the theory, lets you run the game across the world's biggest emitters, then compares the outcome to actual emissions data from 1990 to 2023.

The setup

Each country chooses to abate (cut emissions) or emit (keep polluting). The payoff structure is the prisoner's dilemma:

Country B
AbateEmit
Country A Abate +3, +3 -1, +5
Emit +5, -1 0, 0
Nash equilibrium (rational outcome) Pareto optimum (best for everyone)

The trap: both countries are better off if they both abate (3,3) than if they both emit (0,0). Yet each individual country always does better by emitting. So both rationally choose to defect — and both end up worse off.

Tit-for-tat means conditional cooperation: a country starts by cooperating, then copies what most other countries did last round. If others abate, it keeps abating. If others defect, it defects back. In the game, it represents trust that can survive only when enough countries keep cooperating.

What does the data tell us?

Pure cooperators are rare. Only the EU acted like a sustained "abater" — and it's the bloc with the strongest internal enforcement (carbon markets, binding directives). Cooperation needs institutions to lock it in.

The free-rider strategy paid off short-term. China and India's emit trajectories coincided with massive economic growth. The defector captures the +5 payoff while others sacrifice.

The collective outcome was bad. Despite 33 years of negotiations, atmospheric CO₂ rose from 354 to 421 ppm and global temperature climbed about 1°C. The Nash equilibrium dragged the world toward the lose-lose outcome the model warned about.

Where the model gets it right — and wrong

Right

Defection beating cooperation in one-shot decisions, and drift toward the bad equilibrium without enforcement, is exactly what 33 years of climate negotiations show. The EU's success under binding rules vs. the universal failure of voluntary regimes (Kyoto, Copenhagen) is straight out of the textbook.

Wrong / oversimplified

Real countries don't pick one strategy. They drift, switch on elections, react to oil prices.

Emissions per capita matters morally even if game theory ignores it. India's emissions grew because hundreds of millions of people gained electricity.

Technology changed the payoff matrix. Solar got 90% cheaper between 2010 and 2023. When abating becomes cheaper than emitting, the dilemma dissolves — that's the actual escape hatch.

Model assumptions and simplifications
  • 12 major emitters are modeled explicitly; rest of world is held constant.
  • Policy effects are stylized and deterministic for clarity.
  • Temperature and damage relationships are reduced-form, not an IAM.
  • Historical charts use real data series; forward paths are educational projections.

Methodology

The simulation combines real baseline values with simplified transition rules so users can inspect strategic dynamics quickly. It is designed for intuition and classroom clarity rather than forecast precision.

Data: emissions baselines from Global Carbon Project, atmospheric CO₂ from NOAA Mauna Loa, and warming trend from HadCRUT5/Met Office.

Limits: no sector disaggregation, no uncertainty propagation by default, and no endogenous policy negotiation mechanism.
